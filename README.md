# BCR Repertoire Analysis

This project explores a publicly available B-cell receptor (BCR) repertoire dataset using Python. The goal is to work through the main steps of downstream repertoire analysis and understand how sequence-level features can be connected back to B-cell biology and, ultimately, antibody discovery.

I am particularly interested in how V(D)J usage, CDR3 characteristics, clonotype abundance, clonal expansion, and repertoire diversity can be used together to describe a B-cell population. Where the dataset allows, I will also examine isotype and somatic hypermutation.

The project focuses on downstream analysis of annotated repertoire data rather than building a V(D)J annotation pipeline from raw reads.

---

## Background

B-cell receptors are membrane-bound immunoglobulins expressed on B cells. The antigen-binding portion of the receptor is formed by paired heavy and light chains, and much of the diversity in the B-cell repertoire originates during V(D)J recombination.

For the immunoglobulin heavy chain, variable (V), diversity (D), and joining (J) gene segments are recombined. Light chains undergo V-J recombination. Additional diversity is introduced at the junctions between these segments.

The complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) spans the recombination junction and is generally the most variable part of the receptor. Because of this, CDR3 sequence is particularly useful when examining clonotypes and the structure of an immune repertoire.

Following B-cell activation, somatic hypermutation can introduce additional mutations into immunoglobulin variable regions during affinity maturation. B cells may also undergo class-switch recombination, changing antibody isotype while retaining the same underlying antigen specificity.

High-throughput BCR sequencing makes it possible to examine these features across large populations of B cells rather than individual receptors.

---

## Questions I want to address

Rather than treating the dataset as a collection of sequences alone, I am using the analysis to answer a set of biological questions:

- What does the overall repertoire look like?
- Which V and J genes are most frequently used?
- What is the distribution of CDR3 lengths?
- Which clonotypes are most abundant?
- Is the repertoire dominated by a few expanded clones or distributed across many smaller clones?
- How diverse is the repertoire?
- Are there differences between biological samples or conditions?
- If isotype or somatic mutation information is available, what does it add to the interpretation?
- How could these repertoire features be combined with functional or screening data when evaluating antibody candidates?

- sequencing approach
- whether the data are bulk or single-cell
- relevant experimental conditions

I will preferentially use an AIRR-compatible repertoire table or a similarly structured annotated dataset.

Typical fields of interest include:

- sequence identifier
- V gene call
- D gene call
- J gene call

- CDR3 amino-acid sequence

- clone or clonotype identifier
- clone abundance
- isotype
- mutation information

Large raw sequencing files will not be committed directly to this repository. The original data source and accession information will instead be documented so that the analysis remains reproducible.

## Data inspection and quality control

Before interpreting repertoire biology, I first inspect the structure and quality of the dataset.

This includes checking:

- dimensions of the dataset
- column names and data types
- missing values
- sample identifiers
- consistency of metadata
- productive versus non-productive rearrangements
- missing or ambiguous V(D)J calls
- CDR3 sequence availability
- unusual CDR3 lengths
- duplicate records
- clonotype identifiers
- clone-count distributions
- sequencing depth or number of sequences represented per sample

These checks are important because unusual repertoire results do not necessarily reflect unusual biology. Errors can arise from sample identification, sequencing, annotation, filtering, metadata handling, or downstream analysis.

For raw sequencing data, additional upstream QC would be required before repertoire analysis, including read-quality assessment, adapter removal, and evaluation of the V(D)J annotation output.

## V(D)J gene usage

One of the first repertoire-level analyses is to examine how frequently individual V, D, and J gene segments occur.

I will summarize:

- V-gene usage
- J-gene usage
- V-J combinations
- D-gene usage where appropriate
- gene usage across different samples or conditions, if the dataset supports comparison

Gene usage can help describe the composition of a repertoire and identify receptor families that are disproportionately represented.

However, enrichment of a particular V or J gene is not sufficient on its own to establish antigen specificity. It needs to be interpreted alongside clonotype structure, CDR3 sequence, and experimental context.

---

## CDR3 analysis

CDR3 is particularly useful in repertoire analysis because it contains substantial sequence diversity generated at the V(D)J junction.

I will examine:

- CDR3 amino-acid length
- distribution of CDR3 lengths
- frequently represented CDR3 sequences
- CDR3 characteristics within abundant clonotypes
- differences in CDR3 distributions between samples, where relevant

Where appropriate, nucleotide and amino-acid CDR3 sequences will be considered separately.

CDR3 information will not be interpreted in isolation. It will be considered together with V/J assignments, clonotype abundance, and other available sequence features.

---

## Clonotype analysis

A major goal of repertoire sequencing is to understand the clonal structure of the B-cell population.

The exact definition of a clonotype can vary between studies and analysis pipelines. Depending on the dataset, clonotype assignment may incorporate V and J gene usage, CDR3 sequence or sequence similarity, and paired-chain information.

I will therefore document the clonotype definition used in the selected dataset rather than assuming that all pipelines define clones in the same way.

The analysis will examine:

- number of unique clonotypes
- abundance of individual clonotypes
- most highly represented clones
- clone-frequency distribution
- contribution of dominant clones to the total repertoire
- evidence of clonal expansion

Expansion of a B-cell clone can be biologically interesting, particularly in the context of an immune response, but clone abundance alone does not demonstrate antigen binding or functional activity.

---

## Repertoire diversity

Beyond looking at the largest clones, I also want to describe how evenly sequences are distributed across the repertoire.

Depending on the dataset, I will calculate measures such as:

- clonotype richness
- Shannon diversity
- Simpson diversity
- relative contribution of dominant clones

These measures capture different aspects of repertoire structure.

For example, two samples could contain a similar number of unique clonotypes but differ substantially if one is dominated by a few highly expanded clones while the other contains a more even distribution.

Diversity measures will be interpreted with sequencing depth in mind because samples containing more sequences may appear more diverse simply because they were sampled more extensively.

---

## Somatic hypermutation

If the dataset provides germline comparison or mutation annotations, I will examine somatic hypermutation as an additional repertoire feature.

Possible analyses include:

- mutation frequency across sequences
- mutation burden across clonotypes
- mutation levels among highly expanded clones
- comparison between samples or isotypes

Somatic hypermutation introduces mutations into immunoglobulin variable regions following B-cell activation and is an important component of affinity maturation.

The exact analysis performed will depend on the mutation information available in the selected dataset.

---

## Isotype distribution

Where constant-region information is available, I will also examine the distribution of immunoglobulin isotypes.

These may include:

- IgM
- IgD
- IgG
- IgA
- IgE

Isotype information can provide additional context about B-cell activation and class-switch recombination.

Class switching changes the constant region of the antibody and therefore its effector function, while the antigen-recognition specificity encoded by the variable region is retained.

---

## Bulk and single-cell repertoire data

Bulk BCR sequencing provides a broad view of repertoire composition but generally does not preserve which heavy and light chains originated from the same individual B cell.

Single-cell V(D)J sequencing can retain this pairing information.

Depending on the experimental platform, single-cell data can provide:

- paired heavy- and light-chain sequences
- individual cell barcodes
- cell-specific clonotype assignments
- linkage between receptor sequence and other measurements from the same cell

For 10x Genomics datasets, tools such as Cell Ranger can be used upstream to assemble and annotate V(D)J sequences and assign clonotypes.

If paired single-cell data are used in this project, I will treat heavy/light-chain pairing as an additional biological feature rather than collapsing the analysis to sequence counts alone.

---

## Connecting repertoire analysis to antibody discovery

A BCR repertoire can contain thousands or millions of sequences, but the purpose of an antibody discovery workflow is not simply to identify the most abundant sequence.

Repertoire information can help identify potentially interesting B-cell clones, but candidate prioritization should incorporate additional experimental evidence.

Relevant information may include:

- antigen binding
- specificity
- affinity
- functional activity
- clone abundance
- CDR3 sequence
- V(D)J usage
- somatic mutation
- paired heavy- and light-chain sequence
- sequence diversity
- developability-related characteristics

For this reason, I view repertoire analysis as one part of a larger decision-making process.

If screening data are available, a useful next step would be to connect sequence and clonotype identifiers with experimental results so that repertoire features and antibody function can be evaluated together.

## Troubleshooting unexpected results

One aspect of sequencing analysis that I find particularly important is determining what to do when the computational result does not match the biological expectation.

I would avoid assuming immediately that either the computational analysis or the experiment is responsible.

Instead, I would work through the problem systematically:

1. Confirm sample identities and metadata.
2. Review sequencing and upstream QC.
3. Check filtering criteria and analysis parameters.
4. Examine V(D)J assignments and clonotype definitions.
5. Look for missing data, unusual distributions, or outliers.
6. Compare the computational result with the experimental design.
7. Ask whether the result is biologically plausible.
8. Review relevant aspects of sample preparation and experimental handling.
9. Discuss discrepancies with the experimental team.
10. Determine whether the result is most consistent with biology, technical variation, or an analysis artifact.

The goal is not simply to produce an output from a pipeline, but to understand whether that output makes sense in the context of the experiment.

## Limitations

This project is an exploratory analysis of public BCR repertoire data rather than a complete production antibody-discovery pipeline.

Interpretation may be limited by:

- sequencing depth
- available sample metadata
- upstream V(D)J annotation
- clonotype definitions
- lack of paired heavy/light-chain information in bulk datasets
- lack of antigen-specific screening data
- lack of functional validation

These limitations are important because sequence-level features can identify biologically interesting candidates, but they cannot replace experimental validation.
